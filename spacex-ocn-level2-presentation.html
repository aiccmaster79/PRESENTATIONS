Why This Matters

Getting mass to orbit used to mean throwing away a machine the size of an airliner after one use. SpaceX’s bet was that a first stage that lands turns launch from a firework into an airline: inspect, refuel, fly again.

That is why Starlink can be a dense LEO network, why NASA can buy crew seats commercially, and why IT careers now sit next to RF, software and factory telemetry — not only “space science.”

The IT angle

Constellations are software, ground stations, encryption, and operations dashboards. A satellite is a remote computer with a radio. If you can explain reuse and Starlink, you can talk about cloud-scale systems that happen to live in orbit.

Expendable vs Reusable

Expendable

  • Every stage is hardware you pay for once
  • High cost per kilogram to orbit
  • Simple after MECO — nothing has to fly home
  • Classic model: Saturn V, many still-flying rockets

Reusable (Falcon 9 model)

  • First stage lands, is inspected, and flies again
  • Cost per kilogram drops as flight count rises
  • Needs grid fins, landing legs, extra propellant, recovery ships
  • Second stage is still usually expended

Teaching rule

Reuse is not “free launches.” It is extra hardware and extra burns so the expensive piece — the booster — is not scrap metal in the Atlantic.

Ask who pays for the extra landing fuel. The customer still pays, but less than a new booster. ~5 min.

Starlink — Orbit as a Network

Starlink is a low Earth orbit broadband constellation. Thousands of small satellites hand traffic between space, user dishes, and ground gateways. Latency is lower than geostationary TV satellites because the path is shorter.

Reuse is the industrial trick: you cannot economically refresh a mega-constellation if every launch throws the booster away.

User terminal

Phased-array dish tracks satellites. From the user’s view it is “internet,” not a rocket company.

Trade-offs

Coverage in remote places vs sky brightness, debris risk, and who controls a global network.

Link to 5G and IoT decks: same packet-switching idea, different medium. ~5 min.

Crew Dragon and Cargo

Crew Dragon

  • Carries astronauts to the ISS for NASA
  • Capsule is recovered and can fly again
  • Life support, abort motors, touchscreens
  • Demo-2 (2020) was the first commercial crewed orbital US flight

Cargo Dragon

  • Supplies the ISS — no crew on board
  • Can return experiments to Earth
  • Same family, different certification and seats
  • IT still matters: telemetry, uplinks, ground software
Stress certification: people-rated software is a career path, not a meme. ~4 min.

Case Study: Flight 20, 21 Dec 2015

Falcon 9 Flight 20 Orbcomm OG2 LZ-1, Cape Canaveral

What happened

After delivering 11 Orbcomm satellites, the first stage boosted back, re-entered, and landed vertically at Landing Zone 1. It was the first time an orbital-class booster had been recovered that way.

Why IT students care

The payload was a communications constellation. The landing was the proof that reuse was operational, not a video. Everything after — cadence, Starlink, crew — leans on that proof.

Name the date. Contrast with Grasshopper hops that were suborbital. ~5 min.

Jargon Buster

SpaceX
Space Exploration Technologies Corp. Private launch company founded in 2002.
Falcon 9
Two-stage orbital rocket with a reusable first stage and nine Merlin engines.
MECO
Main Engine Cut-Off — first-stage engines shut down before staging.
RTLS
Return To Launch Site — booster flies back to a ground pad.
ASDS
Autonomous Spaceport Drone Ship — floating landing pad downrange.
Grid fins
Lattice control surfaces that steer the booster during atmospheric descent.
LEO
Low Earth Orbit — a few hundred kilometres up. Starlink’s neighbourhood.
GEO
Geostationary orbit ~36,000 km up. Higher latency than LEO broadband.
Starlink
SpaceX LEO satellite internet constellation and user terminals.
Starship
Fully reusable Super Heavy + ship stack, still in flight test.
Raptor
Methane/oxygen engine family used on Starship and Super Heavy.
Merlin
Kerosene/oxygen engine family used on Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy.
Crew Dragon
Reusable capsule that carries astronauts, certified for NASA crew.
Fairing
Nose shell that protects the payload on the way up; often expended.
GNC
Guidance, Navigation and Control — the software/hardware that flies the vehicle.
Telemetry
Live vehicle data sent to the ground. Operations is an IT job as much as a rocket job.
